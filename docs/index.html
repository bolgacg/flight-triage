Before anything else

Five things to know

This page is about flight logs, so here is what a flight log is and what is being counted, in five sentences. Nothing after this assumes you have seen one.

  1. Every flight of a drone running the open-source PX4 autopilot writes a log: a few hundred thousand rows recording what the sensors measured, what the controller asked for and what the aircraft did, several hundred times a second.
  2. Pilots upload those logs to a public database, review.px4.io, to get plots back. It now holds 463,000 public flights.
  3. When they upload, some of them rate the flight. Crash, software or hardware means the aircraft or its code failed. Crash, pilot means they flew it into something. Good and great mean it came home fine. Those ratings are the only human judgement used on this page.
  4. From each log this study computes indicators: one number per flight for vibration, for how often the position estimator refused to believe a sensor, for how far the aircraft lagged the commands it was given, and so on. Higher always means more worrying.
  5. An indicator only earns its place if, at a fixed tolerance for false alarms, it finds crashed aircraft more often than the check the autopilot already runs by itself.
What happens to one log

Terms, in the order they appear
armed The part of the log where the motors could turn. Everything is measured over that window only, so sitting on a bench does not count.
indicator One number computed from one flight. Higher means more worrying.
false alarm A flight the rule flags that the pilot rated good or great.
detection rate The share of crashed flights the rule flags.
estimator The software that fuses the sensors into one belief about where the aircraft is. When it rejects a measurement it is saying it does not believe that sensor.
test ratio The estimator's own measure of how far a sensor reading sat from what it expected. Above 1.0 means rejected.
failure detector A check PX4 already runs in flight, watching for extreme attitudes, a dead motor and an imbalanced propeller. This study's baseline.
fit half and measured half Every threshold is chosen on one half of the flights and reported on the other, so no number on this page was tuned to the number beside it.
Their words

Why this page and not another

This page answers a gap the centre published about itself six months ago. In the compliance table of its Kenya operations paper, against the safety objective asking whether the aircraft are maintained by a competent entity, the objective is marked not met, and the evaluation reads:

No formal maintenance structure exists. There are no documented maintenance instructions, no dedicated maintenance personnel, and no maintenance log or record-keeping system. Maintenance procedures, staff responsibilities, and training records will be established before future operations. Maalouf and 21 others including Schultz Lundquist, "SORA 2.5-Guided BVLOS UAS for Wildlife Conservation in Kenya: Reducing Friction Between Safety and Field Operations", Drones 2026, 10(3), 178, Table A13, objective 03, published 5 March 2026. doi.org/10.3390/drones10030178

The last sentence is the one that makes this worth building. They are not saying records do not matter; they are saying records are the next thing to put in place.

The same campaign flew "over 260 flights and more than 60 h of UAS activity across the Ol Pejeta Conservancy", with post-flight logging listed as one of the few steps applied consistently to every operation. So the logs exist. What does not exist yet, by their own account, is anything that reads them.

currently, drones lack the technological maturity for widespread application in this field; they are often viewed as unreliable, time-consuming, and expensive for use in ecological experiments and conservation efforts. Schultz Lundquist and others, "WildDrone: autonomous drone technology for monitoring wildlife populations", Frontiers in Robotics and AI 12, 12 January 2026

The sentence in that paper that argues against this page

Their own conclusion on where automation pays says the opposite of what a post-flight tool would like to hear, and it belongs here rather than in a footnote:

Its greatest value lies in planning and pre-flight phases [...] packing, inspection, and post-flight tasks remain largely physical and offer limited scope for automation. Same Kenya paper, on the value of automation

That is true of the physical work, and this page does not touch it. Nobody automates opening an airframe and looking at a motor mount. What is left after the physical work is a folder of logs that were recorded anyway and nobody has time to open, and the question of which ones deserve an hour. That question is arithmetic, and arithmetic is what this page does.

Two more things make the format the right one. The centre's most active public repository, lyrebird, exists to make its DJI aircraft speak "MAVLink 2 on by default, reporting as PX4", so the log shape this page reads is the shape their own bridge was built to produce. And the centre has written a log reader before: AQ-LogReader, for the AutoQuad boards it flew in 2017.

Stated plainly, because it matters for how the page is read: the Kenya fleet was mostly DJI with ArduPilot on the custom builds, and that paper does not mention PX4. The claim here is not that the centre flies PX4 as standard. It is that PX4 and MAVLink logs are the format its own tooling converges on, and that the arithmetic below transfers to any log with the same signals in it.

Act two

A week of logs, in the order a person should read them

Why this matters The job is not to classify one flight. It is to put a pile of logs in an order, so the first ten a person opens are the ten worth opening.

Every flight in the measured half, sorted by how many indicators sit above their threshold. The rating column is hidden until you ask for it, because that is the situation a centre is actually in: the logs arrive, and nobody has said yet which ones went wrong.

Click a row to see that flight's indicators. Reveal turns on the column showing what the pilot said, so you can check the order against the truth.

Act three

What one false alarm in ten is worth

Why this matters A rule that flags everything finds every crash and is useless. The only honest way to compare rules is to give them the same tolerance for false alarms and see what each one finds.

Every threshold on this page is set on the fit half so that the rule flags a fixed share of flights the pilot called good. That share is the budget. The preregistered budget is one in ten; the slider shows the rest of the family, and those other points were not fixed in advance.

Drag it. Left is a stricter rule that misses more; right is a noisier rule that catches more. The thresholds were set to spend 10 percent of the budget on the fit half, and the figure above is what they actually cost on the measured half, which is the number that counts. Every other point on the slider was produced after the fact and is exploration.

Act four

The test this page could have failed

Why this matters A rule that flags every flight that ended badly is not reading the machine, it is reading the outcome. The cohort contains a group that separates the two, and it was chosen before any of this was computed.

Some pilots rated their crash as their own fault. On those flights the aircraft may have been perfectly healthy right up to the moment it met the ground. An indicator that measures machine health should do clearly worse there than on hardware and software failures. If it does just as well on both, it is detecting bad endings, not bad aircraft.

Now hide the ending

Every flight here ends on the ground one way or another, and an impact writes vibration and tracking error into the log whatever caused it. So the whole study was run a second time on the same flights with the last seconds before the end thrown away. If the rule still finds crashed aircraft with the ending hidden, it is reading trouble rather than reading the landing.

Where each indicator actually stands

Every indicator, at the preregistered budget, measured on the half of the flights that chose none of the thresholds. Coverage is the share of flights whose logs carried what the indicator needs; a low number there is a limitation, not a result.

How it was built

Method, limits and sources

This page checking itself

The headline could have been typed in by hand and nobody would know. So the page ships the per-flight numbers it was built from, re-applies the rule to them in your browser right now, and prints what it gets beside what the build script got.

Running.

The split, and why it is there

Every flight was put in one of two halves by the first hexadecimal digit of the md5 hash of its log id: even chooses thresholds, odd reports them. Nothing about the flight's contents or its rating enters that decision. The fit half is where each indicator's threshold was set so that it flags the budgeted share of healthy flights. The measured half was read once, after the rules were fixed, and every later read of it is listed in PREREGISTRATION.md.

What was fitted, in one box

Nothing here is a trained network. What was fitted is a set of thresholds, and a threshold fitted to data is still a model, so it gets the same card.

The cohort

What the numbers are made of

The logs in this cohort span PX4 v1.3 to v1.18, roughly a decade of firmware, with a handful reporting no version at all and two reporting a version that does not exist, and the topics available changed several times over that period. So the first tier of indicators is computed here from raw signals every generation records: the accelerometer, the estimator's own test ratios, the rate the controller asked for against the rate the airframe produced, the battery voltage and current, and the satellite fix. Vibration, for example, is the accelerometer magnitude minus its half-second rolling mean, reported as the 95th percentile of the per-second standard deviation of what is left. The second tier exists only on newer firmware and includes the autopilot's own failure detector, which is the baseline every first-tier indicator has to beat.

Limits, stated before you find them

The ratings are what the uploader chose, not an investigation. A pilot who blames themselves may be wrong, and a pilot who blames the aircraft may be wrong in the other direction. The groups are noisy in a way that no amount of computation fixes.

The cohort is drawn from flights people chose to upload, which is not a random sample of flying. Logs from a bad day are more likely to be uploaded than logs from an ordinary one.

Nothing here is a maintenance recommendation. The page ranks logs for a human to open; it does not say a part is failing, and it has no access to what was actually found afterwards.
